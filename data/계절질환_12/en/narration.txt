Rainy day fatigue isn't laziness, it's your hormones and blood vessels reacting to the weather. When cloudy, low-pressure days stack up, plenty of people feel drained and can't figure out why, but there are real physical reasons behind it. First, less sunlight means your body produces less serotonin, the hormone that keeps you feeling alert and motivated. Second, when barometric pressure drops before a storm, blood vessels in your brain dilate and release a burst of serotonin, and it's that swing, not the rain itself, that studies link to headaches and brain fog. One analysis found nearly three in four migraine sufferers name falling pressure as their top weather trigger. Third, low pressure also nudges your body toward rest mode, dropping your blood pressure and leaving you sluggish even after a full night's sleep. To fight back, get outside or sit near a bright window first thing in the morning, even under clouds, since light exposure is still the strongest serotonin trigger you have. Keep a consistent sleep and wake time so your body clock doesn't drift, and stay ahead with light exercise, which helps stabilize your mood on the gloomiest days.
