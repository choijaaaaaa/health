Patulous Eustachian Tube, could a diuretic or your birth control pill be behind that strange echo in your ear? If your own voice or breathing sounds oddly loud inside your ear, the cause isn't always what you'd expect. The Eustachian tube, the passage connecting your middle ear to the back of your nose, is supposed to stay closed most of the time. When it stays open instead, sound travels straight through to your eardrum, so your own voice echoes and your ear can feel full or clogged.

Weight loss is the risk factor doctors mention most often for this condition. A fat pad around the Eustachian tube normally helps hold it closed, and losing weight quickly thins that padding out. Among patients after bariatric surgery, roughly one in five report symptoms that match it.

There's a second cause that's less talked about: certain medications. Diuretics and estrogen-containing birth control pills have both been linked to a patulous Eustachian tube, since they change fluid balance in a way that can thin the tissue lining the tube. If your symptoms started around the same time as a new prescription, that timing is worth mentioning to your doctor.

Stress and exercise tend to make symptoms worse, while lying down or bending forward often brings quick relief. That's because blood flow shifts toward your head in that position, swelling the tissue around the tube just enough to narrow it again.

If you're on a diuretic or birth control pill, ask your doctor whether an alternative makes sense. Saline nasal spray used with your head tilted down is worth trying for quick relief, and it's worth booking a visit with an ENT doctor if symptoms keep affecting your daily life.
