If you play soccer, basketball, or volleyball, there's a good chance you've heard about someone tearing their ACL — and if you're a female athlete, your risk is a lot higher than you might think. Your ACL connects your thigh bone to your shin bone and keeps your knee from twisting too far. Plant your foot, pivot hard, or land from a jump the wrong way, and it can tear. Most people who tear it feel or hear a pop in that exact moment.

Here's the part that surprises people: female athletes are up to eight times more likely to tear an ACL than male athletes in the same sport. At the last two Women's World Cups, twelve and a half percent of players had already had ACL reconstruction surgery before the tournament even started — nearly double the rate among the men. Researchers point to a few reasons for this gap. Women's ACLs tend to be smaller, and the bony notch the ligament passes through is often narrower, which leaves less room before it's stressed to the point of tearing. Hip and knee alignment differs too, and hormonal shifts during the menstrual cycle appear to add to the risk.

Most ACL tears happen with no contact from another player at all — just a bad landing or a sudden turn. Cutting, pivoting, jumping, and landing are the exact movements that show up constantly in soccer, basketball, and volleyball, which is exactly why these sports carry such a high rate of ACL injuries.

Two things matter most here: knowing how to respond right if an injury happens, and training that changes how you land and pivot so it's less likely to happen in the first place.

First, protect the knee right away if you suspect a tear. Stay off it, ice it, and keep it elevated above your heart. Don't just wait out the pain and hope it settles down — get it evaluated within days, not weeks, since delaying treatment raises the risk of additional damage to the cartilage inside the knee.

Second, train how you land and pivot before you ever get hurt. Structured neuromuscular training programs have cut ACL injury rates by up to eighty-eight percent in young female soccer players in published studies. The training itself isn't complicated — strengthening your quads and hamstrings gives your knee more support, and practicing landing softly with your knee tracking over your toes, instead of caving inward, takes a lot of stress off the ligament.

Build landing and strength training into your routine, and your ACL tear risk drops significantly. And if a pop, sudden swelling, or a knee that feels like it's giving way happens after a pivot or landing, get it checked out right away instead of waiting it out.
