Diabetic peripheral neuropathy - the CDC estimates about 7 million American adults are living with this nerve damage. Years of high blood sugar damage both the nerves themselves and the tiny blood vessels that feed them, so numbness and tingling usually start in the toes and fingers first. Two things speed up that damage, and once the numbness sets in, one more thing turns it dangerous.

First, blood sugar that stays poorly controlled for years - the longer you've had diabetes and the higher your average blood sugar has run, the higher your risk climbs. Second, high blood pressure and heart disease. Research on people with diabetes has found that hypertension and cardiovascular disease are linked to a higher risk of nerve damage, on top of blood sugar alone.

Here's why the numbness itself turns dangerous. Once you can't feel your feet, small cuts and blisters go unnoticed and untreated. Up to a third of people with diabetes develop a foot ulcer at some point. Ulcers from nerve damage alone see roughly an 11 percent amputation rate, but that jumps to around 29 percent when blood flow to the foot is also poor.

Keep your blood sugar in your target range, and manage blood pressure and cholesterol right alongside it. Then check your feet every single day for cuts or blisters - catching a small wound early is what keeps it from turning into something serious.
