Diverticulitis — think it's only your grandparents' problem? Think again.

Among Americans under 50 hospitalized with complicated diverticulitis, the share jumped from about 18.5% in 2005 to 28.2% in 2020. Doctors are now seeing this condition show up in people decades younger than they used to expect.

So what's actually driving it, and who's most at risk? A few things stand out: diet, gender, and an old myth that's still scaring people away from healthy foods.

Diverticula are small pouches that bulge out through weak spots in your colon wall, and a low-fiber, red-meat-heavy diet is the single biggest driver — it causes constipation, which raises pressure inside the colon and pushes those weak spots outward. Women are affected more than men, with rates of roughly 228 per 100,000 versus 118 per 100,000 after age 40. Watch for sharp pain low in your left abdomen, fever, chills, or bloating.

Here's the myth: for decades, doctors told patients to avoid nuts, seeds, corn, and popcorn, worried they'd get stuck in a diverticulum and trigger inflammation. Harvard, Mayo Clinic, and Cleveland Clinic have all walked that back — research actually links eating more nuts and popcorn to a lower risk, not a higher one.

What actually lowers your risk is fiber, water, and movement. Aim for 25 to 35 grams of fiber a day from vegetables, fruit, whole grains, and legumes — that alone cuts risk by around 40% compared with a low-fiber diet. Drink enough water alongside it, since fiber doesn't work well without it, and add vigorous exercise like jogging to keep your colon moving.

Diverticulitis is climbing fastest among younger adults, and diet is the main reason why. Skip the fear around nuts and seeds, and put your energy into fiber, hydration, and exercise instead.
