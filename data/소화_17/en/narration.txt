If milk or ice cream leaves you bloated, gassy, or running to the bathroom, you're not imagining it — that could be lactose intolerance. The NIH estimates that thirty to fifty million Americans deal with it, and it hits some groups far harder than others: up to ninety percent of Asian Americans and eighty percent of Black Americans have it, compared to as little as fifteen percent of people with Northern European ancestry. It comes down to lactase, the enzyme your small intestine uses to break down lactose, the sugar in dairy. When you're not making enough lactase, that undigested lactose travels straight to your colon, where gut bacteria ferment it and produce the gas, cramping, and diarrhea. Three changes make the biggest difference: the type of dairy you choose, how you eat it, and replacing the enzyme you're missing.

First, the type of dairy matters. Fresh milk keeps its lactose fully intact. But hard, aged cheeses like cheddar, parmesan, gouda, and gruyere test at under a tenth of a gram of lactose per serving, because the bacteria used in aging consume it over time. Reach for Greek yogurt or an aged cheese before regular milk.

Second, how you eat it changes everything. Your body can only break down so much lactose at once, so a full glass on an empty stomach overwhelms it fast. Spread your dairy across the day instead of one big serving, pair it with other food, and skip drinking it on an empty stomach.

Third, replace the missing enzyme. Lactase supplements work by supplying the enzyme your gut isn't making enough of. Take one to three caplets right with your first bite of dairy, and each dose covers roughly thirty to forty-five minutes of eating.

Switching up the type of dairy you eat and how you eat it makes lactose intolerance a lot easier to live with. But if you're losing weight without trying or seeing blood in your stool, that points to something beyond lactose intolerance — see a doctor to find the real cause.
