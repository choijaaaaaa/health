Trigger finger happens when the tendon that bends your finger swells up inside the narrow tunnel it passes through. A small nodule forms on the tendon, and it catches every time you bend or straighten the finger — that's the locking or popping feeling people describe.

If you have diabetes, your risk is a lot higher. Research out of Japan found that people with diabetes are up to four times more likely to develop trigger finger than people without it, and doctors also see it more often in people over 45 and in people with rheumatoid arthritis.

It's not just diabetes, though. Repetitive gripping is a major trigger too — heavy manual work, tools that require a strong grip, and even long hours on a keyboard and mouse can irritate that tendon sheath over time.

Here's how to spot it: the finger gets stuck in a bent position and may snap straight with a pop. It's usually worse first thing in the morning and eases up as you move it through the day. The ring finger and thumb are hit the most, and about one in three people end up with it in more than one finger.

Treatment follows a clear ladder. For mild cases, an over-the-counter anti-inflammatory for about two weeks, plus gentle finger stretches, is often enough.

If the finger is actually locking, a night splint that keeps it straight while you sleep is the next step — no needles, no surgery.

When it keeps catching despite that, a steroid injection into the tendon sheath works for most people and calms the swelling directly. Surgery only comes in when none of that helps, and it's a quick outpatient procedure. Don't just wait it out — if your finger is locking, get it checked within weeks, not months.
