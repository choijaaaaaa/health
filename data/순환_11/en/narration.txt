If your Apple Watch just flagged a low heart rate, or you've been feeling unusually tired and lightheaded, don't brush it off. A resting heart rate under 60 beats per minute is called bradycardia.

Not all bradycardia is dangerous. If you exercise regularly, your heart pumps more blood with every beat, so a resting rate in the 40s or 50s can simply mean your heart is working efficiently - that's actually a sign of good cardiovascular fitness. The real concern shows up when a slow pulse comes with dizziness, fatigue, or fainting, even without an athletic lifestyle. Three everyday factors are worth checking first: sleep apnea, medication, and vagal overstimulation.

Sleep apnea comes first. When your airway repeatedly collapses during sleep, the resulting drop in oxygen triggers an overactive vagal response that pulls your heart rate down. Loud snoring or waking up exhausted even after a full night's sleep are signs worth paying attention to. Sleeping on your side instead of your back keeps your airway more open, and losing excess weight reduces pressure on it. If snoring is severe, ask your doctor about a sleep study.

Medication is second. Beta-blockers and calcium channel blockers, commonly prescribed for high blood pressure or arrhythmias, are designed to slow your heart down - and if the dose isn't quite right for your body, that effect can go further than intended. Never stop a prescribed medication on your own. If you notice your pulse has dropped noticeably, tell your doctor so the dose can be reviewed.

Third is vagal overstimulation. Straining hard during a bowel movement, splashing your face with cold water, or standing still in one place for a long time, like waiting in a long line, can trigger a strong vagal reflex that drops your heart rate suddenly. Getting enough fiber and water makes straining less necessary, and if you have to stand still for a while, shifting your weight or flexing your calves keeps blood from pooling in your legs.

Managing sleep apnea, checking in with your doctor about medication, and easing off habits that overstimulate the vagus nerve all help stabilize your pulse. An underactive thyroid is another common cause of a slow heart rate, so if you're also feeling unusually cold or gaining weight, ask your doctor to check your thyroid levels at your next checkup. That said, if your heart rate drops below 40 or you experience repeated dizziness or fainting, seek medical care right away.
