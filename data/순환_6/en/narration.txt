Raynaud's phenomenon affects 3 to 5 percent of people, and most don't realize a few overlooked factors make attacks worse.

Ordinary cold hands stay uniformly cold and pale. Raynaud's is different - the small arteries in your fingers or toes overreact to cold or stress and spasm shut suddenly, so skin color changes in a sharp, well-defined pattern: white first, then blue or purple, then red once blood flow returns. Episodes can last minutes to over an hour.

Three factors matter most: caffeine and other stimulants, nicotine, and low magnesium.

Caffeine and other stimulants come first. Coffee, energy drinks, and chocolate all narrow blood vessels, and stimulant medications used for conditions like ADHD can do the same - something patient groups say gets missed constantly. Cutting back to one or two cups a day, and asking your doctor whether a medication is contributing, are both worth trying.

Nicotine is second. It is actually a stronger vasoconstrictor than caffeine, yet it is often overlooked as a trigger. Smoking narrows finger blood vessels even further, making attacks both more frequent and more intense. Cutting back one cigarette at a time, or talking to your doctor about a quit plan, noticeably lowers how often attacks happen.

Low magnesium is third, and it works differently - it is something to add rather than cut. Magnesium helps blood vessels relax, and studies found lower magnesium levels in women with Raynaud's than in those without it. Nuts, leafy greens, and whole grains are simple ways to close that gap.

One more tool worth knowing: thermal biofeedback, which trains you to consciously raise your finger temperature. In one clinical study, about 57 percent of patients reported feeling better a year later. Combined with cutting caffeine and nicotine, it meaningfully reduces how often attacks happen. That said, if color changes are severe or you notice skin sores, it could point to an underlying condition - worth a doctor visit.
