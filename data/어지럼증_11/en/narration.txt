Medication-related dizziness — if it started after a new prescription, save this before you forget. The pills you're taking could be part of the problem.

Falls are the leading cause of injury-related death among older adults in the US, according to the CDC. Certain drug classes are especially likely to trigger dizziness: blood pressure medications, benzodiazepines and sleep aids, and antihistamines. Taking five or more medications at once — a pattern doctors call polypharmacy — raises the risk even further.

First, blood pressure medications. They can cause orthostatic hypotension, a sudden drop in blood pressure when you stand up, leaving you lightheaded or briefly faint.

Sit on the edge of the bed or chair for a few seconds before standing, especially first thing in the morning or after using the bathroom. Rising slowly gives your blood pressure time to catch up.

Second, benzodiazepines and sleep aids. These can linger in your system well into the next day, slowing your reactions and leaving you drowsy or unsteady on your feet.

Move carefully and turn on the lights during nighttime bathroom trips. If grogginess lasts into the morning, ask your doctor whether an earlier dose or a shorter-acting option makes sense for you.

Third, antihistamines. Older, first-generation antihistamines cross into the brain and can cause both drowsiness and dizziness as a side effect.

Ask your pharmacist about switching to a non-drowsy, second-generation antihistamine. If you've already taken a first-generation one, hold off on driving or climbing stairs until the effect wears off.

Rising slowly for blood pressure medications, adjusting timing for sleep aids, and switching to non-drowsy antihistamines — these three habits noticeably cut medication-related dizziness. If you're taking five or more medications, ask your doctor or pharmacist for a full medication review. And if dizziness starts after a new prescription, don't just stop taking it — talk to the provider who prescribed it first.
