PMS, most of what circulates online about managing it is guesswork — here's what randomized trials and ACOG guidelines actually back.

Researchers have traced part of the mood symptoms to a drop in allopregnanolone, a byproduct of progesterone, during the luteal phase — the two weeks before your period. Lower levels of this compound are linked to more anxiety and irritability, while shifts in aldosterone cause the body to hold onto sodium and water, which shows up as bloating. Skipping calcium-rich foods, taking high-dose vitamin B6 without checking the amount, and skipping exercise all make the physical and mood symptoms harder to manage.

First is skipping calcium-rich foods. Clinical trials point to about twelve hundred milligrams of calcium a day, roughly three to four servings of dairy or fortified alternatives, as one of the better-supported ways to ease both the physical and mood symptoms of PMS. Spreading that across meals works better than one large dose at once.

Second is taking high-dose vitamin B6 supplements without checking the label. The evidence for B6 helping PMS symptoms is mixed, and doses much above one hundred milligrams a day have been linked to nerve damage over time. If you try it, stick to a lower dose and check the label rather than assuming more is better.

Third is skipping exercise because you feel too drained to move. Regular aerobic activity, even just brisk walking most days, consistently eases fatigue and low mood tied to PMS, likely by supporting the same brain chemicals that dip during the luteal phase.

Prioritizing calcium-rich meals, keeping vitamin B6 doses in check, and staying active through the week before your period make the physical and mood symptoms far more manageable. If symptoms are severe enough to disrupt work or relationships most months, that crosses into PMDD territory, which affects roughly three to eight percent of women of reproductive age, and it's worth bringing up with an OB-GYN.
