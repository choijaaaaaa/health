Not every wisdom tooth needs to come out - but the difference matters, so check this before you decide anything. Dentists used to pull them out as a precaution just in case. Current guidance says that's not automatic anymore: if there's no infection and no damage to the teeth around it, removal isn't necessary.

So what actually forces the decision? A flap of gum tissue often covers part of an emerging wisdom tooth, and food and bacteria get trapped underneath it. That's pericoronitis - throbbing pain, bad breath, and a gum that looks red and puffy, sometimes bad enough that opening your mouth gets hard.

There's a second trigger too. When the tooth comes in tilted instead of upright, it leans on the molar right next to it. That constant pressure can wear down the neighboring tooth's root and open up a gap where cavities form.

Either one of those - infection that keeps returning, or damage to the tooth beside it - is what tips the decision toward extraction, and it's simplest in your early twenties. No infection, a clean bite, easy to brush around? Regular dental checkups are enough.

While you're figuring out which one you're dealing with: warm salt water rinses and a cold compress for 20 minutes at a time take the edge off. But if the pain sticks around past a day or two, or you're having trouble opening your mouth or swallowing, that's your sign to get seen - don't just ride it out.
