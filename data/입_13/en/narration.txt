Tonsil stones — clinically called tonsilloliths — are small, hardened lumps that form inside the pockets on your tonsils called crypts. They're made of trapped food debris, dead cells, and bacteria that mix with minerals like calcium and slowly calcify into a whitish or yellowish lump. Most tonsil stones are tiny, but even a grain-sized one can smell surprisingly strong when you press on it, and many people describe the odor as sulfuric or rotten-egg-like. Bad breath is by far the most common symptom, though larger stones can also cause a scratchy throat, a feeling that something is stuck, or even referred pain in the ear.

Frequent tonsillitis is one of the biggest risk factors. Repeated tonsil infections cause the crypts to widen and deepen over time, giving debris more room to collect and get trapped. If you get tonsillitis often, treating those infections promptly and gently rinsing the back of your throat with warm salt water a couple of times a day helps keep the crypts from filling up in the first place.

Dehydration and dry mouth play a role too. Saliva naturally rinses bacteria out of your mouth and throat, so when you're not drinking enough water, or you tend to breathe through your mouth while you sleep, that natural cleansing slows down and bacteria build up faster inside the crypts. Staying hydrated throughout the day and addressing chronic mouth breathing, including nasal congestion or allergies that force it, makes a real difference.

Poor oral hygiene rounds out the list. Leftover food particles are the raw material tonsil stones are made of, so brushing your teeth, flossing, and rinsing the back of your throat after meals reduces what's available to get trapped in the crypts in the first place.

Most tonsil stones can be managed at home and aren't a sign of anything serious. Resist the urge to dig them out with your finger or a cotton swab — that can scrape the tonsil tissue and leave it more prone to trapping debris again. If stones keep coming back despite good hygiene, or you're dealing with ongoing throat discomfort, an ENT doctor can offer more targeted options, from a gentle in-office suction removal to a procedure called cryptolysis that flattens the crypts for people with chronic, recurring stones.
