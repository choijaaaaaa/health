If cold air, dry air, or a sudden weather change makes your nose run or clog up — even though your allergy tests come back negative — you might be dealing with vasomotor rhinitis, not a typical allergy.

Vasomotor rhinitis isn't triggered by a specific allergen at all. The blood vessels inside your nasal lining just overreact to nonspecific triggers instead. Temperature swings, dry indoor air, and strong smells are three of the most common ones.

First, temperature swings. Stepping from a heated or air-conditioned room into the opposite temperature outside makes the vessels in your nasal lining suddenly dilate, and congestion or a runny nose can hit within minutes.

Layering up before you go outside, and covering your nose and mouth with a scarf in cold weather, gives your nasal lining time to adjust instead of reacting all at once.

Second, dry indoor air from heating or air conditioning dries out the nasal lining, which makes it even more reactive to every other trigger on this list.

Running a humidifier to keep indoor humidity between 40 and 60 percent, and using a saline nasal spray, keeps the lining from drying out in the first place.

Third, strong smells like perfume, cleaning products, or cigarette smoke, along with stress and sudden emotional shifts, can set off the exact same nerve response — with no allergen involved at all.

Regular allergy medication often doesn't touch this kind of rhinitis, which is exactly why it gets missed for so long. Doctors more often reach for azelastine, an intranasal antihistamine, or budesonide, a nasal steroid spray, both of which are approved specifically for nonallergic rhinitis, along with an ipratropium bromide spray for a persistently runny nose.

Buffering temperature swings, keeping indoor air humid, and steering clear of your specific triggers cut down flare-ups noticeably. If congestion or a runny nose sticks around for more than a few weeks despite that, talk to an ENT about a targeted nasal spray instead of reaching for another allergy pill that was never going to work in the first place.
