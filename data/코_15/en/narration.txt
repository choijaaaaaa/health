Allergic rhinitis — if your sneezing and runny nose just got worse this month, here's why. Ragweed season is starting right now, and there's a good chance you've been treating it wrong. Here's what's actually happening and what genuinely helps.

Ragweed pollen allergy affects more than 23 million people in the US, and nearly 50 million experience symptoms from it in late summer and fall. It's responsible for about half of all pollen-related allergic rhinitis cases in North America. Ragweed season begins in early August, peaks in mid-September through October, and tapers off after the first frost.

One thing you've probably heard doesn't actually work: local honey. The idea is that eating local honey builds tolerance to the pollen making you sneeze. But bees collect large, heavy flower pollen that barely becomes airborne, while ragweed pollen is small and wind-blown. It's a completely different pollen. A controlled study found daily local honey gave no real improvement over a placebo.

Here's the mechanism. When ragweed pollen lands on your nasal lining, your immune system misreads it as a threat and releases histamine. Histamine widens blood vessels and irritates nerve endings, which is why you get sneezing, a runny nose, congestion, and itchy eyes all at once.

Three things genuinely help. Nasal saline irrigation, a HEPA air filter used the right way, and knowing when an antihistamine is worth it.

First, nasal saline irrigation. Hypertonic saline rinses physically flush pollen out of your nasal passages, and studies show they lower nasal symptom scores and cut how much rescue antihistamine people need.

Second, a HEPA air filter, but keep your expectations realistic. It reduces airborne particles by close to 70%, which helps your bedroom air overall, though some studies found it didn't fully eliminate sneezing and congestion on its own. Pair it with saline rinses rather than relying on it alone.

Third, an over-the-counter non-drowsy antihistamine works well for most people. If symptoms last past a few weeks despite that, or you're missing sleep over it, see a doctor about a prescription nasal steroid spray instead of just pushing through it.

Nasal saline irrigation, a HEPA filter as backup, and the right antihistamine genuinely cut ragweed symptoms down. If your symptoms drag on for over a month or start affecting your sleep and focus, don't just wait it out — see an allergist and get tested for what's actually triggering it.
